1. Introduction to golf ball physics


The first, and perhaps most crucial, step in understanding golf ball flight is applying Newton's laws of motion. This begins with the impact phase and the concept of impulse, which is defined as the product of force and time. Impulse explains how the forces acting on the ball affect its initial take-off by relating the momentum of the golf club before impact to the total momentum after impact. Specifically, the impulse applied by the club equals the ball's change in momentum, characterized by the equation $I = mv - mu$.

After the club applies this impulse, the ball begins its flight through the air, at which point the principles of projectile motion come into play. In an idealized system with no air resistance, the gravitational force felt by the ball acts strictly vertically downwards. As a result, gravity only alters the ball's vertical velocity; its horizontal velocity remains completely constant.

Applying these rules to a simulation model results in a simple parabolic trajectory that is perfectly symmetrical about the apex of the ball's flight. However, this symmetrical flight path only occurs because we have not yet accounted for aerodynamic forces, such as drag and the Magnus effect, which I will discuss further in the next educational box to the right.


2. The magnus effect and other refinements to validity of the projectile motion model


After defining the key physical concepts that govern golf ball motion, we must consider the effects of air resistance. Air resistance, also known as drag, acts in the opposite direction to the ball's motion. It is the result of frictional forces as the object moves through a fluid, such as air. Consequently, the ball experiences both horizontal and vertical drag components, which is a primary reason why a golf ball's flight trajectory is not perfectly symmetrical when viewed from the side.

The next physical phenomenon to consider is the Magnus effect, which occurs when a spinning object moves through a fluid. In golf, a ball struck with a lob wedge can spin at up to 10,000 rpm. This spin generates a force perpendicular to the ball's direction of motion, explaining why shots curve left, right, higher, or lower depending on the axis of rotation.

This perpendicular force arises from a pressure difference across the ball. The side of the ball rotating in the same direction as the airflow accelerates the surrounding air, creating a region of lower pressure. Conversely, the side rotating against the airflow slows the air down, creating a region of higher pressure. This pressure difference exerts a resultant force perpendicular to the direction of motion, driving the Magnus effect.


3. This project and an introduction to how it works and its applications


This project had the initial aim to provide an interactive simulation for golf ball trajectories and provide a platform for all users. The main features were to be able to provide accurate, precise data while also being realistic and taking into consideration many factors present in real-life golf, like aerodynamic drag and the Magnus effect.

The process of development for this project undertook a similar path to the one you have just taken reading this educational content. It started out as a rudimentary model which used something called Euler's method to calculate the ball's next position based on its current one and its velocities. This simulation provided a simple, symmetrical parabola emulating the bare-bones flight path.

After this, the project evolved in complexity, adding more variables such as drag, which is dependent on the speed of the ball at any given time. After this, I added the RK4 method, which takes an average of four slopes to improve the accuracy of the Euler method.

This finally led me to the final and most challenging improvement of the model, which was the application of the Magnus effect and the spin of the ball. This required complex calculations and even some theoretical trigonometry to be able to determine the forces on the ball due to it spinning.

Some first ideas for applications of this project were to be used as a modelling tool to aid golf club manufacturers in the design process, but during development, I have realised that this can be used as a tool for all to better understand the physics behind golf ball flight.
